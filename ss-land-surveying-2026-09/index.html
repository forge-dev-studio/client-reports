Leads have risen every single month since the site went live: 35, 36, 57, 76, 91. August alone produced more leads than the first two months combined, and the site now appears in Google search results about eleven times as often each day as it did in its first two weeks. 297 people have submitted the form since April 15, and 12 of them have already come back with another project.

What people ask for

Boundary surveys are 71 percent of everything that comes in: 212 of the 297 inquiries. The rest splits across other and general requests (39), parcel work (13), ALTA surveys (10), construction staking (10), topographic (8) and site plans (5).

What carried it

The home page has earned 374 search clicks, the most of any page. The guide on what a land survey costs in Georgia is next at 165 clicks from 18,000 appearances, followed by the guides on reading a survey plat, finding your property lines and subdividing land. People also search for the firm by name: the top queries of the whole period are all variations of S&S Land Surveying, which together account for 93 clicks. A growing share of people are looking for this firm specifically, not just any surveyor.

The Business Profile this week

The Google Business Profile stands at 4.9 stars across 58 reviews. This week we completed a full pass on the profile itself: the website link now points to the secure address, ten services are listed instead of two, a book-online link now goes straight to the contact page, the year established is on file, and the service-area list was cleaned up and extended to the towns the site actually serves, which is awaiting Google review. Two profile posts Google had quietly declined were rewritten and are live, and the two scheduled for September were corrected before they publish.

Paid traffic against everything else

Paid brought slightly more visits; unpaid traffic produced more results from fewer of them. The two also behave differently: form submissions ran 24 to 10 in favor of unpaid traffic, while phone taps ran 18 to 13 in favor of paid. People arriving from an ad tend to call, and people arriving from search results tend to fill out the form.

As of September 1 the website stamps every new lead with the traffic source that produced it, right on the contact record. From here forward this split comes from the customer records themselves rather than from analytics sampling, so the September report will show exactly which leads came from ads and which came from search, by name.

Two different engines produce this footprint. We measured Google visibility from 134 points across the region: in the map pack, the block of businesses beside the map, S&S appears at every point within ten miles and at 25 of the 26 points between ten and twenty miles, then thins out through the twenties and is gone by forty. Proximity rules that surface. Ordinary search results stretch the footprint differently; they are weaker close to home but still turning up at thirty and forty miles, where the pack has stopped. That is the website doing exactly what it was built to do: four out of five inquiries come from within 60 miles, well beyond what the map pack alone would ever deliver.

Beyond roughly 60 miles the picture changes. Organic visibility thins out there, yet one inquiry in five still comes from that range, and Google Ads is the engine with reach into it. Over June through August, ads bought 1,213 visits against 702 from search results, and ads can be pointed at any geography regardless of how Google ranks the site there. The practical division of labor: the website owns the core service area, and ads extend it.

Next steps

For discussion September 2026

  1. Connect the Quo phone system to the CRM

    Today, calls are the blind spot: analytics counts taps on the phone number, but no call ever becomes a record we can follow up on. Connecting Quo puts every call into the customer system alongside the form leads.

    That unlocks the two campaigns we want to run: seasonal text campaigns to past customers, and automatic review requests sent the moment a job wraps up, which is what grows the review count from 58 without anyone having to remember to ask.

  2. Pick the trigger for review requests

    The request should go out when the job is done. The cleanest trigger is the calendar: when the appointment for the fieldwork passes, the request sends. Alternatives worth discussing: marking the job complete in the CRM (one tap, most accurate), or sending on invoice or payment (fires exactly when the customer is happiest or least happy, depending on the job). We can start with one and change it later.

  3. Put pricing back on the survey cost pages

    The cost guide is the single most read piece of content on the site, 165 clicks from 18,000 appearances, and it currently answers the question without numbers. Restoring price ranges to the cost guides gives those readers the answer they searched for and filters out callers whose budget was never close.

  4. The surveyor referral program

    Covered above: one firm per market, 80 mile protected territories, and a free month for every introduction that turns into a signed firm. The ask for this meeting is simple: look at the candidate list and tell us who Bart knows.

  5. Link the Google Ads account, view only

    The ads run independently and that does not change: we would not manage or touch the campaigns. A view only link lets the reporting show ad spend context next to results, ties ad clicks to the leads they produce, and completes the paid side of the analytics, which currently flags the account as unlinked. Ten minutes of granting access, no operational change.

  6. Complete the customer list

    To finish the customer database, we need first name, last name and email for each client sold since April 15. That backfills the records behind the older inquiries, and it is the list the seasonal campaigns and review requests in step 1 will actually send to. A simple spreadsheet works; we will handle the import.
