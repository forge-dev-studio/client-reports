What a buyer finds today

We ran the search a specifier would run. Here is the result, in order.

Search Carter Custom Carpets and the first result is your Facebook page. Below it: Bloomberg, Yelp, MapQuest, LinkedIn, ZoomInfo, and a customs shipping records site listing your import history.

Every result on that page is a third party describing Carter. Not one of them is Carter.

Google has also started writing its own summary answer at the top of that page, assembled from those same directory listings, because there is no primary source for it to use instead. Whatever those directories got wrong is now the answer.

The name is a second problem. Search Carter carpets and both the first result and Google's business panel belong to Carter Carpets and Vinyl, a flooring store in Temperance, Michigan, on a domain one letter away from yours. Carter Custom Carpets appears twelfth, through Facebook again.

That search happens after something else already worked. A rep mentioned you. A designer found an old specification sheet. About recovering the old site

You asked whether it could be brought back. We went through every saved copy in the Internet Archive, 165 pages going back to 1998.

It should not be restored. From early 2024 onward, every saved copy carries advertising spam injected into Carter's own pages, hidden runs of unrelated keywords attached to your real text. Someone had access to the site and was using it to host content that had nothing to do with your business. That is also a likely reason it went offline, because hosts suspend accounts serving spam.

We recovered everything worth keeping from those pages and used it. The rest stays in the archive where it belongs.

The strategy

Own the moment of specification.

Not advertising. Not chasing shoppers. Carter does not sell to the person browsing for a rug, and it should not try.

The buyer who matters has already heard your name and is deciding whether to specify you. A designer settling a color and a width. A purchasing office confirming a supplier exists. A showroom needing the real construction detail behind a product they sell under their own label.

Win that moment and everything upstream gets easier. Your reps stop sending folders of attachments and start sending a link. Your customers' salespeople can answer a specification question without calling Rome. And the supplier diversity programs that hotel groups and federal buyers run have a place to point.

That is the whole strategy, and it happens to be the one thing a website is genuinely good at.
